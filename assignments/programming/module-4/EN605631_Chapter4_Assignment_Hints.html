EN.605.631 — Chapter 4 Assignment Hints (Discrete Random Variables)

Use these hints to organize your approach without jumping straight to final numbers. Pair each hint with a quick sketch/table and, when useful, a tiny simulation to sanity‑check.

Quick roadmap

  • Name the random variable precisely and state its support.

  • Decide whether to work with pmf or cdf. For inequalities, cdf often simplifies algebra.

  • For expectations, default to LOTUS: E[g(X)] = sum g(x) p(x).

  • When a story matches a named family (Binomial/Poisson/Geometric/NegBin/Hypergeo), use that family's known facts.

  • Always check edge cases (min/max of the support) and that probabilities sum to 1.

Problem 1 — Discrete RV: pmf, cdf, plots, and checks (10 pts)

  • Define X in words and list its support. Sanity‑check the endpoints from the story.
  • Build a small table for the pmf; confirm sum_x p(x) = 1.
  • Plot: pmf as bars; cdf as a right‑continuous step (jumps = pmf values).
  • Verify monotonicity/right‑continuity of the cdf; compute two or three probabilities both ways (pmf sum vs cdf difference).

Problem 2 — LOTUS and transformations (12 pts)

  • Identify the function g and compute E[g(X)] with LOTUS rather than deriving the distribution of g(X).
  • If Y = aX + b, use linearity and scaling: , Var(Y) = a^2 Var(X).
  • Check whether g preserves support bounds or introduces new values; handle piecewise g carefully.

Problem 3 — Binomial vs Poisson (12 pts)

  • Decide if the count is Binomial (fixed n, independent trials, success prob p) or Poisson (counts in a window).
  • Compare mean/variance: Binomial has Var = np(1-p); Poisson has Var = mean = λ.
  • If asked for a Poisson approximation to Binomial, ensure n large and p small with np = λ fixed; quantify the approximation error if requested.

Problem 4 — Geometric & its memoryless property (14 pts)

  • Recall geometric pmf for trial index of first success: P(X=k) = (1-p)^(k-1) p, k=1,2,….
  • Prove memorylessness: P(X>m+n | X>m) = P(X>n); use it to simplify conditional questions.
  • Distinguish between the two parameterizations (trials‑to‑first‑success vs failures‑before‑first‑success).

Problem 5 — Negative Binomial as “r-th success” (10 pts)

  • Interpret X as the trial index of the r‑th success. Negative Binomial pmf: P(X=k) = C(k-1, r-1) p^r (1-p)^(k-r), k=r,r+1,….
  • Use E[X]=r/p, Var(X)=r(1-p)/p^2 when appropriate.
  • For conditioning (e.g., at least one success already), rewrite in terms of a shifted geometric/NegBin and reuse known forms.

Problem 6 — Hypergeometric and finite-population correction (12 pts)

  • Recognize sampling without replacement from a finite population → Hypergeometric.
  • Hypergeo mean/var: , Var(X) = n (K/N) (1-K/N) * (N-n)/(N-1) (finite‑population correction).
  • When deriving probabilities, use combinations and keep track of each term’s denominator C(N, n).

Problem 7 — Working directly with the cdf (10 pts)

  • Translate probability statements into cdf differences: P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) - F(a) for discrete X (with right‑continuity).
  • If you’re given/asked for the cdf shape, recover the pmf by jumps: p(x) = F(x) - F(x-).
  • Draw the step function and mark the jump sizes before doing algebra—many inequalities become obvious visually.

Problem 8 — Expectation of a sum (indicator method) (10 pts)

  • Introduce indicator variables I_i for each basic success; then X = sum I_i.
  • Use linearity of expectation: even when the I_i are dependent.
  • If independence holds, you may also compute Var(X) via sums of variances and covariances (state what independence you used).

Common pitfalls

  • Forgetting to state the support or including impossible values.

  • Confusing with replacement (Binomial) and without replacement (Hypergeometric).

  • Using independence where it’s not given; remember linearity of expectation needs no independence.

  • Plotting the cdf as connected lines instead of a right‑continuous step for discrete variables.
